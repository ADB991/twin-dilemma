LLM agents in the twin prisoner's dilemma

Do language models acausally coordinate with a copy of themselves?

You probably know the prisoner's dilemma. Whatever the other player does, you score more by defecting: defection is the dominant strategy, and mutual defection is the Nash equilibrium. But how would you play if the other player were your twin, an exact copy of you, reasoning through the same problem at the same moment?

How often each model cooperates

Nine models each played a one-shot prisoner's dilemma 25 times per condition: five open-weight models run locally, and four Claude models run through the Claude Code CLI. In three conditions the opponent is described as an exact copy of the model, and in the control it is another AI system. The four Claude models cooperate with their copy in 299 of 300 samples, and Qwen 3.8 27B in 62 of 75. Gemma 3 27B (the same size as Qwen) cooperates in 7 of 75, and the three small models almost never. Against another AI system, Haiku and Sonnet always defect, while Opus and Fable cooperate about half the time, on the grounds that another AI probably reasons as they do.

What two samples earn against each other

Each answer is one draw from the model's answer distribution. Pair two independent draws from a cell that cooperates at rate p and each player expects 3p² + 5p(1−p) + (1−p)² = 1 + 3p − p² points. The curve rises all the way to p = 1, so the policy that always cooperates does best against itself even when the two draws are independent. The Qwen answers that defect in the sampling condition reason about a single draw; this curve is the argument at the level of the shared distribution.

How the reasoning was labelled

Two keyword flags per answer: one for the dominance argument ("regardless of what…", "no matter what", "dominant strategy") and one for the correlation argument ("same reasoning", "mirror", "symmetric", "whatever I choose…"). The label is the argument that supports the final choice. A cooperating answer with the correlation flag is correlation, a defecting answer with the dominance flag is dominance, and everything else is other. Claude labelled 88 answers by hand, drawn at random within each model and label (48 from the four local models first run, 32 from the Claude models, 8 from Gemma 3 27B), and compared.

    Caveats

    • One wording per condition, with a fixed order of payoff rows and answer options. Other wordings could move the numbers. The copy claim is only a claim, and the model has no way to check it.
    • The twin prisoner's dilemma is widely discussed online. The models that cooperate may be reproducing the functional decision theory position from their training text; the near-identical rebuttals in the dominance condition look like a learned script.
    • The Claude calls went through the Claude Code CLI, so each carried about 900 tokens of harness context: a one-line system prompt ("You are a Claude agent, built on Anthropic's Claude Agent SDK."), an environment block that names the model, and an account reminder. The API no longer accepts a temperature or a seed, so those calls use its default sampling. Thinking was adaptive at effort low: Haiku thought briefly on every call, Sonnet on 22 of 100, Opus on 30, Fable on none.
    • 25 samples per cell. The twin against control gaps for Qwen, Haiku and Sonnet are far outside the noise (Fisher exact p < 10⁻⁷); Qwen twin against sampling is p = 0.05.
    • The local models ran at temperature 1.0 with fixed seeds and thinking off, so their reasoning is the visible answer. Stated reasoning may rationalise the choice rather than cause it.
    • Gemma 3 27B, the same size as Qwen 3.8 27B, never cooperates with its twin, so size alone does not produce the effect. With one model of each, family and training stay confounded.
    • Local weights are quantised (nvfp4 for Qwen, Q4_K_M for the others). No answer was ever paired with another; the pair payoffs are computed from the rates.
